Adjustment rules

You, with the commercial hat on

The percentages the system is allowed to apply

Blocks pricing

This is the mechanism that stops the AI inventing a number. It can only ever apply a percentage that exists in this table, cited by name. If you write a note saying "add some contingency" and no matching rule exists, it asks you rather than picking something that seems about right.

Each rule needs a plain-English trigger and a number. Three are already scaffolded with invented values — replace them:

Rule When it applies — your words Value
min_quote_value The floor below which you will not quote at all £ ______
rush_uplift how many days counts as rush? ______ ____ %
difficult_job_contingency what makes a job "difficult"? ______ ____ %

What else do you routinely add? Programming charge, first-off inspection, carriage, a minimum line value, a new-customer margin, a long-standing customer discount. Anything you find yourself adding by habit should be a rule, so it gets applied consistently and shows on the quote as its own line.

Rules only flag, never price

Worth knowing

A rule can be set to raise a flag for a human rather than change a number — useful for "thin wall, go and look at this" cases where the response is judgement, not a percentage. Tell me the ones you'd want and I'll set them up that way.

D

Material and stock

You / your buyer

The stock sizes you actually buy

Only for full-supply jobs

For jobs where you supply the material, the system works out how many blanks come out of a piece of stock and how many pieces to buy. That calculation is plain arithmetic, not a judgement call — but it needs to know what you buy. If most of your work is service-only on customer free-issue material, this can wait.

Send me — a spreadsheet is ideal, one row per size
  • Material spec, exactly as you'd write it — 1.2312, EN24T
  • Form — plate, round bar, square bar, billet
  • Dimensions in mm (length × width × thickness, or length × diameter)
  • Cost per piece
  • Saw / grip allowance you leave around each blank

If a material has no stock listed, the system refuses to price it and flags it for your buyer rather than inventing a material cost.

This got easier. Where a supplier publishes their range on a web page, the system can now read the sizes off it and keep the list current on its own — see Where to look up prices below. What you send me is then the starting point rather than a list somebody has to maintain by hand.

How much you leave on for machining

Blocks correct material sizing

You told me the rule of thumb: 3–5mm on the OD, and 3–5mm on the length of one part. That is now what decides the size of bar a job buys, and it needs one number each rather than a range. Until they are set, the calculator sizes stock to the finished part — which buys a bar with nothing left to clean up — and flags every job saying so.

Tell me two numbers
  • Millimetres left on the diameter, or on each face of a square section
  • Millimetres left on the length of one part, before the parting cut

Worth knowing how it uses them, because it changes what gets bought: it grows the part by your allowance first, then takes the smallest size your supplier actually holds. So a part needing Ø89 buys the Ø90 bar — not Ø89, which nobody sells. Both numbers live in the rules table and you can change them yourself; they are a data edit, not a code change.

Where to look up prices

Blocks live material costs

Steel moves. A system that reads your prices once at install and never again is wrong within a month, quietly, in whichever direction the market went. So it goes and looks: it fetches a page, reads the figure off it, and keeps the line of text it read alongside the number. Nothing is ever recalled from memory — if a page cannot be reached, the price shows as unread and the job flags, rather than falling back to something plausible.

Send me, for each material you buy regularly
  • The web address of the page that actually shows the price — your usual stockholder's product page is ideal
  • Whether that price is your account price or the public one
  • The material spec and form it covers — EN16 round bar, EN30B square, and so on

Eight sources are already set up and switched off, waiting for addresses: EN16, EN30B, 1.2312, 6082 aluminium, 316 stainless, EDM wire, electricity and a trade hourly-rate benchmark. You can paste the addresses in yourself under Market data in the app.

Two honest limits. Some trade sites hide prices behind a login or draw them with JavaScript — those cannot be read this way, and the system will say so plainly rather than guess. And published web prices are small-quantity retail; buying on account you will pay less, so treat a scraped figure as a ceiling until you tell me your actual account rate.

Densities, if you want prices per kilo to work

Mostly done already

Stockholders price per kilogram; the calculator needs to buy a bar. Converting between the two needs the material's density, and there is deliberately no default — guessing a density to reach a price is exactly the kind of invention the rest of this system exists to prevent. Steels are already filled in at 7,850 kg/m³. If you quote much aluminium, bronze or plastics, I need theirs, or those materials fall back to a typed price and flag.

Linking the scanned back catalogue

Phase two — but decide the route now

You asked how scanned historic quotes get linked in. Honest answer: the two look-up lanes — "have we cut this shape before" and "has this kind of job hurt us before" — search structured rows, not images. A scanned PDF sitting in a folder cannot be matched on tolerance band or envelope. So the question is how we get from paper to rows, and there are three routes:

Route 1 — export, if your current system can If quotes live in a system that can export to CSV or Excel, this is by far the best route. A few thousand rows imported in an afternoon, and the lanes start working immediately.
Route 2 — type in the ones that matter Not everything. The repeat parts and the jobs that went wrong are where the value is; a one-off from 2019 you never saw again earns nothing. A hundred good rows beat three thousand poor ones.
Route 3 — OCR the scans Feasible, and the same vision pipeline that reads drawings can read a quote sheet. But it is its own project with its own accuracy problem, and I would not start it until the live system is earning its keep.

Tell me what form the back catalogue is actually in and I will tell you which of these is worth doing. Until then, note that the archive compounds — it is thin at first and gets genuinely useful over months, so it is not a reason to delay going live.

Recording won and lost

Habit to start on day one

When a quote comes back, someone marks it won or lost — and where you know it, the time the job actually took. Without that the system knows what it quoted but never whether it was right, and it cannot improve. It is two clicks and it is the difference between an archive and a filing cabinet.

Industry knowledge loop

You, ongoing

Notes are how your judgement gets in

How it improves

When an estimator types "we already have the electrode for this, drop 15 minutes", the system turns that into a specific change to a specific operation, reprices from the changed input, and stores the note with the price before and after. Every one is a recorded example of judgement the AI could not reach alone.

Nothing is learned silently. When the same note keeps recurring, it appears on the rules screen as a candidate — and a person decides whether it becomes a standing rule. The system never promotes its own rules.

Decide
  • Who reviews the candidates — you, or a senior estimator?
  • How often — monthly is usually about right

Decisions I need from you

Nobody else can make these

May it suggest processes the customer didn't ask for?

If a customer says "sparking", the system quotes sparking and nothing else — that's settled and I wouldn't change it. The open question is the other case: the customer says nothing, and the drawing plainly needs milling before wire. Should it propose the full routing, or only ever quote exactly what was asked? It currently proposes, and marks the proposal clearly.

How sure must it be before it shows you a value?

Every field it reads carries a confidence score, and below a threshold the value is withheld and shown as unread rather than as a number. Too high and estimators retype everything; too low and wrong values slip through. I've set sensible starting points — material 85%, tolerance 90%, quantity 90% — but the honest answer is that we tune these from the scoring exercise rather than guess now.

Who is allowed to approve a quote?

Anyone who can log in, or a named few? Approval is the moment the system stops being a draft and becomes a price you stand behind, and it's recorded with the person's name against it.

When a quote comes back for revision, new version or edit in place?

A sent quote is frozen and revising starts version 2, which keeps the history clean. If you'd rather amend and resend under one number because that's what customers expect, tell me — but I'd argue for keeping versions.

Nothing here needs to happen at once. Rates, the mailbox and the scoring drawings get you to a working system on real numbers. Everything else improves it from there.

Every figure currently in the system is an invented placeholder — the £38/h spark erode, the margins, the stock costs, all of it. The seeding script refuses to run against a production database for exactly that reason. Real rates come from you before anything is quoted to a customer.
